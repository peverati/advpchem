Experiment 8: Stability Analysis and Transition Metals

Is your SCF solution really the ground state?

Excitation energies of Fe and Ru+ with and without SCF stability analysis.

At a glance. Five functionals on the Fe and Ru⁺ states, each run twice (without and with stability analysis), def2-TZVP: 24 jobs. You will learn what an unstable SCF solution is, how to detect and fix it, and why transition metals need extra care.

Background

As you know, the coefficients of the basis functions in the Kohn–Sham orbitals come from an iterative procedure, the self-consistent field (SCF) procedure. Starting from an initial set of coefficients (the initial density matrix, or “guess”), it produces the final, optimized coefficients, which computational chemists call the converged SCF solution.

A converged SCF solution is a stationary point, but we can’t know in advance whether it is a minimum, a maximum or a saddle point. To be sure we have the lowest energy, we must characterize the stationary point and correct it if needed. This stability analysis requires the second derivatives of the energy with respect to orbital rotations (the electronic Hessian), and it can be repeated more than once. When an instability is detected, two constraints can be lifted to lower the energy (Seeger and Pople 1977): we can change the theoretical framework (from restricted to unrestricted), or we can allow complex instead of real orbitals. The wave function obtained after a stability analysis is stable and has a lower energy than the unstable one.

How can an SCF end on an unstable solution? For example, it can land on an excited state while you are computing the ground state. With a reasonable guess this is unlikely for molecules made mostly of main-group elements, but it is much more likely for systems with nearly degenerate low-lying states, such as transition-metal atoms. Without a stability analysis you would never notice. Good descriptions of the procedure are in Seeger and Pople (1977), Bauernschmitt and Ahlrichs (1996) and Fukutome (1981).

In this experiment you will use five functionals with the def2-TZVP basis set (Weigend and Ahlrichs 2005): PBE (Perdew et al. 1996) for both atoms, BP86 (Becke 1988; Perdew 1986a, 1986b) and PBE0 (Adamo and Barone 1999; Ernzerhof and Scuseria 1999) for Ru⁺, and M06-2X (Zhao and Truhlar 2008) and B97M-V (Mardirossian and Head-Gordon 2015; Vydrov and Van Voorhis 2010) for Fe. These are the combinations whose calculations converge reliably both with and without stability analysis. Other functionals (SPW92, B3LYP, SCAN, ωB97M-V) and Hartree–Fock were left out because their SCF often fails to converge for these atoms, itself a lesson about transition metals. Ru needs an effective core potential (ECP = def2-ECP).

You will compute the excitation energies of two atoms, Fe (reference 34.32 kcal/mol) and Ru⁺ (reference 21.96 kcal/mol). The data come from the 3dAEE8 (Hoyer et al. 2014; Luo et al. 2014; Yu and Truhlar 2014) and 4dAEE5 (Luo and Truhlar 2012) subsets of the Minnesota 2015B database (Yu et al. 2016). Run two sets of calculations:

  1. Without stability analysis: compute the excitation energy E(excited) − E(ground).
  2. With stability analysis: add INTERNAL_STABILITY TRUE to the $rem section, and check in the output whether an instability was found and the wave function re-optimized.

As usual, compute the errors with respect to the reference values.

TipTip

This simple example shows that calculations involving transition metals are not easy. Even for “reactions” between atomic states the errors can be very large, and in extreme cases (Cao and Ryde 2019) it isn’t clear which functionals give the right answer. As general advice, always run a stability analysis on the final solution. It is mandatory for systems with low-lying states, such as transition metals, especially with a small basis set. In these problematic cases, it is always a good idea to seek help from a theoretician.
